1 Context and Scope

Context Domain: International relations, geopolitical analysis, and defense economics.

Topic: A deep-dive exploration of state-level interactions, focusing on the historical triad of military expenditure, bilateral trade volume, and conflict involvement using the Correlates of War (COW) and SIPRI datasets.

Topical Aspects Addressed: How do specific states’ economic and military profiles change before, during, and after a selected conflict? What does the trade and conflict network look like for a specific country over a chosen decade?

Questions Answered: “What were the economic and military dynamics between [Country A] and [Country B] during [Conflict X]?” “How has a specific nation’s military spending correlated with its trade network over time?”

Expected Benefits: Providing an unopinionated, flexible tool for researchers and analysts to generate hypotheses and cross-reference information from different data sources.

Publication: A dedicated web-based dashboard (built via Streamlit), optimized for desktop use due to the density of information.

2 Project Objectives and Success Criteria

Objectives:

  • Build a robust exploratory dashboard that allows users to query the data by either “State” (e.g., viewing all of France’s trade and conflicts) or by “Conflict” (e.g., viewing all participants and their spending during the Vietnam War).
  • Implement linked views and reactive filters so that adjusting a timeframe or selecting a country automatically updates all relevant charts and data tables.

Success Criteria:

  • Qualitative: Users report the dashboard effectively replaces manual cross-referencing of CSV files; researchers find the interface intuitive for hypothesis generation.
  • Quantitative: High interaction depth (e.g., average of >10 clicks or filter adjustments per session)
  • Out of Scope: Pre-written narratives, editorializing the data, predictive machine-learning models forecasting future wars, and real-time conflict tracking (as datasets rely on historical collection).

4 4 Stakeholder Analysis

The following stakeholders have a direct or indirect interest in the Conflict & Trade Explorer. Their needs and expectations shaped the design decisions throughout the project.

4.1 Project Sponsor / Research Institute

HIGH INFLUENCE HIGH INTEREST

The research institute wants a tool that demonstrates what modern data visualisation can do for security studies — something that goes beyond static charts in PDF reports. A well-designed, publicly accessible dashboard reflects positively on the institution, drives traffic to their data infrastructure, and makes a concrete argument for continued investment in data-driven policy research. They expect the product to be academically credible, properly sourced, and presentable to external partners and funders. Their influence on the project is high: they define the context in which the tool is deployed and the audience it needs to reach.

4.2 Data Providers — SIPRI & COW

LOW INFLUENCE HIGH INTEREST

SIPRI and the Correlates of War project make their datasets publicly available for academic and non-commercial use. They have no direct control over how we build the dashboard, but they have a strong interest in seeing their data used correctly — with proper attribution, within the scope of their licensing terms, and without misrepresentation of what the data does or does not cover. Getting this right matters: the credibility of the tool depends entirely on the credibility of the underlying sources. Any visual that obscures data limitations or overstates coverage would reflect poorly on both the providers and the project.

4.3 Target Audience — Policy Analysts, Academics & Researchers

LOW INFLUENCE HIGH INTEREST

This group does not shape the product directly, but they are the reason it exists. Policy analysts need to move fast — a briefing doesn’t wait for a data pipeline. Academic researchers need to trust what they are looking at and be able to trace findings back to a primary source. Both groups share a frustration with the current landscape: the data is public, but access to it requires technical skills that most domain experts simply do not have. The dashboard exists to close that gap. Their interest is high because a tool that genuinely works for them saves significant time and opens up lines of analysis that would otherwise require a data engineering team.

4.4 Design & Development Team

HIGH INFLUENCE HIGH INTEREST

The team building the product has the most direct control over what gets built and how. Technical constraints — dataset size, browser performance, entity matching between COW and SIPRI country codes — directly shape what is feasible within the project timeline. The team has to balance academic rigour with usability: every design decision from the choice of chart type to how missing data is signalled involves a trade-off between completeness and clarity. High influence comes with responsibility — the team sets the standard for what the tool claims to do and must ensure those claims are honest.

  • 2Scans the conflict list for the relevant time period, selects a Conflict ID
  • 3Reads the military expenditure chart — looks for the spike relative to the conflict period shading
  • 4Checks the trade chart to see if the economic relationship was affected
  • 5Screenshots or exports the chart for her briefing document
→ Design Implication

The conflict browser must allow filtering by state pair and year range. Charts must be immediately readable — shaded conflict periods and clear axis labels are not optional extras, they are the core deliverable for this user.

Goals
  • Get a visual narrative of how a conflict evolved and which trade relationships were affected — in under five minutes
  • Compare military expenditure and bilateral trade for any country pair across a defined time window
  • Export a chart directly into a report without reformatting
Frustrations
  • Every new question means starting over: download, clean, merge, plot — by which point the deadline has passed
  • Tools built for data scientists assume she has an afternoon; she has twenty minutes
  • When time series have gaps she can't tell if data is missing or trade genuinely stopped — that distinction matters enormously

Sarah opens one tab, selects a country pair and a year range, and has a defensible, source-backed visual in under five minutes. She walks into the room prepared. For once, the data kept up with her.

6 Situation Assessment

Available Resources: Three robust public datasets (COW Bilateral Trade, COW War Data, SIPRI Military Expenditure). Python ecosystem (Pandas for data wrangling, Streamlit for UI, Plotly for interactive visualization).

Constraints:
- Temporal Mismatch: SIPRI data generally covers post-WWII, while COW data goes back to 1816. The complete triad can only be shown for overlapping years.
- Entity Matching: COW uses distinct numerical “State Codes” which must be meticulously mapped to standard ISO country codes to align with SIPRI data.
- Screen Real Estate: Exploratory dashboards require space for filters and multiple charts; mobile optimization is a secondary priority.

Risks: * Missing Data: Trade data for certain years/countries may be missing.
* Performance: Loading massive bilateral trade CSVs repeatedly will cause lag. Data must be heavily cached and pre-processed.

7 Visualization Concept

The product will rely heavily on the concept of “Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand.”

7.1 Product Form

A single-page, exploratory dashboard built with Streamlit.

7.2 Visual Encodings

  • Macro-Timeline and/or Key Metrics: A broad brush-able timeline of global military spending and total active conflicts.
  • Relational Network: If a state is selected, a network graph shows trading partners and enemies. If a conflict is selected, it shows the belligerents.
  • Comparative Line Charts: Detailed, multi-axis line charts showing the selected states’ military expenditure (line) vs. trade volume (bar/area) over the selected time period. Active war years are shaded.

7.3 Interactivity

  • Entity Selector: A toggle allowing users to search by “State” or “Conflict.”
  • Global Time Slider (if possible): Adjusting the slider dynamically filters all charts on the canvas.

7.4 Narrative and Annotation

  • Text elements are restricted to clear chart titles, axis labels, and tooltips containing exact figures.
  • Disclaimers regarding data gaps or definitions (e.g., “intra-state” vs “inter-state”) are accessible via info-icons.

7.5 Target Medium and Integration

A standalone web application, strictly optimized for desktop/laptop use to accommodate the complex multi-view interface.

7.6 Value Articulation

  • Cognitive and analytical value: By allowing users to pivot instantly between a “Conflict-centric” view and a “State-centric” view, the design matches a researcher’s natural workflow. The juxtaposition of shaded war timelines over trade/spending lines amplifies pattern detection.
  • Communicative value: It acts as a neutral, objective tool. The data speaks for itself based on user-defined parameters.
  • Experiential and aesthetic value: A highly functional, clean “software” aesthetic builds trust. Clear visual cues for missing data ensure academic rigor and user confidence.
